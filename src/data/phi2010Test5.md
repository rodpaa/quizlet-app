# Title: PHI 2010 Test 5 Study Guide

Description: App-ready review of freedom of action, free will, determinism, compatibilism, incompatibilism, moral responsibility, punishment, and the meaning of life. Based only on the supplied lecture notes, draft study guide, and assigned readings.

Subject: Philosophy

## Vocab

1. Freedom of Action: The ability to act on your will without an external impediment.
2. External Impediment: An obstacle outside the agent that blocks action, such as physical restraint, coercive force or threats, or insufficient means.
3. Free Will: The deeper freedom, beyond freedom of action, that is required for moral responsibility.
4. Will (Frankfurt): The first-order desire that actually moves a person to act.
5. First-Order Desire: A desire to perform an action.
6. Second-Order Desire: A desire about a first-order desire.
7. Type 1 Second-Order Desire: A desire merely to have a certain first-order desire.
8. Second-Order Volition: A desire that a particular first-order desire be one's will and move one to act.
9. Person (Frankfurt): A being capable of having second-order volitions.
10. Wanton (Frankfurt): A being with first-order desires but no second-order volitions; a wanton does not care which desire becomes its will.
11. Higher-Order Desire Theory (HDT): The view that free will is the power to act on the desire one wants to act on, or to have one's will controlled by one's second-order volition.
12. Unwilling Addict: Frankfurt's addict who takes the drug because the desire to take it is effective, while wanting the desire not to take it to be his will.
13. Willing Addict: Frankfurt's addict who takes the drug and wants the desire to take it to be his will.
14. Deep Self View: A view according to which a person is free and responsible when the person's will is controlled by the person's deep self.
15. Sanity (Wolf): The ability to know what one is doing and to know whether what one is doing is right or wrong.
16. Sane Deep Self View: The view that a person has free will when the person's deep self controls the will and the person is sane.
17. JoJo Case: Wolf's case of the favorite son of an evil dictator, raised to accept his father's brutal values, whose brutal will is endorsed by his deep self but who is not morally responsible because he lacks the relevant sanity.
18. Compatibilism: The view that determinism is compatible with free will.
19. Incompatibilism: The view that if determinism is true, then we have no free will.
20. Libertarian Incompatibilism: The view that determinism is incompatible with free will, that free will exists, and therefore that determinism is false.
21. Hard Incompatibilism: The view that we lack the free will required for moral responsibility whether determinism is true or false.
22. State of the World at a Time: A complete list of (i) every object that exists at that time and (ii) **NEEDS CONFIRMATION: the second item is blank in the supplied lecture notes and draft study guide.**
23. Law of Nature: A general law that determines what continuations of a state of the world are physically possible; a more exact class definition was not supplied.
24. Determinism: The thesis that the state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, necessitates or guarantees a unique state of the world at the next time.
25. Conditional Necessity: The necessity of an event given specified earlier conditions; it does not mean the event had to occur no matter what.
26. Garden of Forking Paths: An image of deliberation in which more than one future appears open and it is up to the agent which path is taken.
27. Ability to Do Otherwise Condition (AO): A person performs an action freely only if the person could have done otherwise than that action.
28. Untouchable Fact: A fact that no human being in history has ever had the power to change, regardless of knowledge or luck.
29. Untouchability Principle: If it is an untouchable fact that p, and an untouchable fact that if p then q, then it is an untouchable fact that q.
30. DINO: The lecture abbreviation for the state of the world at an arbitrary moment in 70 million BCE together with the laws of nature.
31. Consequence Argument: Van Inwagen's argument that, if determinism is true, present actions are untouchable consequences of the distant past and laws of nature, and thus fail AO.
32. Self-Forming Action (SFA): A psychologically torn decision through which an agent forms dispositions and character traits.
33. Businesswoman Case: Kane's example of an agent torn between stopping to help an assault victim and continuing to an important business meeting; either effort can prevail in a self-forming choice.
34. Ultimate Responsibility: Kane's idea that agents must be responsible for something that is a sufficient cause or motive of an action, which requires responsibility for earlier self-forming actions that shaped them.
35. Plural Voluntary Control: Kane's claim that, in an SFA, the agent can voluntarily, intentionally, and rationally bring about either competing choice, even though the choice is undetermined.
36. Harmful Consequences Objection: The objection that widespread acceptance of hard incompatibilism would produce harmful moral and social consequences.
37. Theory of Punishment: A general justification for punishing wrongdoers.
38. Retributivist Theory of Punishment: The view that criminal punishment is justified because criminals deserve punishment for their crimes.
39. Contagion Theory of Punishment: Pereboom's view that restraint can be justified, like quarantine, to prevent a dangerous person from further harming society rather than because the person deserves suffering.
40. Moral Responsibility: The kind of accountability that would make a person genuinely deserving of blame or punishment; hard incompatibilism denies that humans have it.
41. Myth of Sisyphus: The story in which Sisyphus is condemned forever to push a stone uphill, only for it to roll back down, producing endless and fruitless repetition.
42. Meaninglessness (Taylor): Endless pointlessness in which activity repeatedly comes to nothing.
43. Objective Meaning: Meaning supplied by a significant culmination or product of activity when considered independently of the agent's attitude.
44. Subjective Meaning (Taylor): Meaning that comes from an agent's inner desire or will to pursue an activity.
45. Sisyphus on Drugs: Taylor's altered case in which the gods give Sisyphus an irresistible desire to roll stones, so that the activity fulfills exactly what he wants.
46. Active Engagement (Wolf): Being gripped, excited, interested, or absorbed by what one is doing rather than passive or alienated.
47. Projects of Worth (Wolf): Projects whose value is not merely subjective; Wolf argues that meaningful lives are actively engaged in projects of positive value.
48. Blob: Wolf's image of a passive life spent idly before a television, used as a paradigm of meaninglessness.
49. Alienated Life: Wolf's case of activity performed without identification or engagement, illustrating that mere activity is insufficient for meaning.
50. Useless Project: Wolf's case of engaged activity directed toward something lacking positive value, illustrating that subjective fulfillment alone is insufficient for meaning.

## Comprehension Questions

1. Question: What is freedom of action?
   Answer: It is the ability to act on one's will without an external impediment such as restraint, coercion, or lack of means.

2. Question: Why is freedom of action not sufficient for moral responsibility?
   Answer: A person's will may itself result from hypnosis, compulsion, brainwashing, mind control, or addiction. The person may act without external restraint while lacking the deeper control required for responsibility.

3. Question: How does Hobbes characterize liberty?
   Answer: Liberty is the absence of external opposition or impediments to motion. His definition therefore focuses on freedom of action.

4. Question: What is the precise course formulation of determinism?
   Answer: The state of the world at any time plus the laws of nature necessitate or guarantee a unique state of the world at the next time.

5. Question: What does determinism mean in ordinary language?
   Answer: Once the total condition of the world and the laws are fixed, only one physically possible continuation can occur.

6. Question: Why does determinism not imply predictability?
   Answer: Determinism is about what the state of the world and laws guarantee, not about what human beings can know or calculate.

7. Question: Why is the coin-toss objection to determinism flawed?
   Answer: Not knowing whether the coin will land heads or tails shows unpredictability for the speaker, not that the outcome lacks determining conditions.

8. Question: How can determinism allow contingency?
   Answer: An event can be necessary given the actual prior state and laws without being necessary "full stop." Different prior conditions could have produced a different event.

9. Question: What is compatibilism, and why are Frankfurt and Wolf compatibilists?
   Answer: Compatibilism says determinism and free will can coexist. Frankfurt locates freedom in alignment between will and second-order volition, while Wolf adds deep-self control and sanity; neither requires an undetermined choice.

10. Question: What is the difference between first- and second-order desires?
    Answer: A first-order desire is directed at acting, such as wanting to study or take a drug. A second-order desire is directed at a first-order desire, such as wanting to want to study or wanting the desire not to take a drug to become effective.

11. Question: Which first-order desire counts as a person's will?
    Answer: The first-order desire that actually causes or moves the person to act.

12. Question: How does a Type 1 second-order desire differ from a second-order volition?
    Answer: Type 1 is merely a desire to have a desire. A second-order volition is a desire that a first-order desire become one's will and actually move one to act.

13. Question: What role do second-order volitions play in Frankfurt's account?
    Answer: They mark personhood because persons care which desire becomes their will. Free will occurs when the effective will is the will selected by the person's second-order volition.

14. Question: Why can persons have free will while wantons cannot?
    Answer: Persons have second-order volitions and can care about and govern which first-order desire becomes effective. Wantons have no second-order volitions, so they cannot have the will they want in Frankfurt's sense.

15. Question: Is Chet free if he takes a drug while wanting his desire not to take it to be his will?
    Answer: No. His effective will is the desire to take the drug, but his second-order volition favors the desire not to take it. He does not act on the desire he wants to act on.

16. Question: How do the Simple Desire Theory and Frankfurt's theory assess the Unwilling Addict?
    Answer: A simple theory that identifies freedom with acting on one's desire would count him free because he acts on the desire to take the drug. Frankfurt counts him unfree because that desire is not the one he wants to be his will.

17. Question: What is a Deep Self View, and why is Frankfurt's theory one?
    Answer: A Deep Self View makes responsibility depend on the will's reflecting the agent's deeper values, identifications, or higher-order desires. Frankfurt's theory does this by requiring the will to match a second-order volition.

18. Question: What are the key details of Wolf's JoJo case?
    Answer: JoJo is the favorite son of an evil dictator and is raised to treat his father's brutal conduct as normal. He rules brutally, wants to do so, and deeply endorses that will, yet his upbringing prevents him from properly recognizing its wrongness.

19. Question: Why does JoJo challenge unsupplemented Deep Self Views?
    Answer: His actions flow from a will endorsed by his deep self, so those views appear to classify him as responsible. Wolf argues that he is not responsible because the deep self controlling him is itself insane.

20. Question: How does Wolf revise the Deep Self View?
    Answer: She requires both deep-self control and sanity. This blocks JoJo-style counterexamples because endorsement is insufficient when the agent cannot understand or appreciate right and wrong.

21. Question: What are Wolf's two course conditions for sanity, and which does JoJo fail?
    Answer: A sane person knows what they are doing and knows whether it is right or wrong. JoJo knows what he is doing but fails the right-or-wrong condition.

22. Question: What are the two main forms of incompatibilism?
    Answer: Libertarian incompatibilism says determinism excludes free will but humans have free will, so determinism is false. Hard incompatibilism says humans lack responsibility-making free will whether determinism is true or false.

23. Question: What does the garden of forking paths represent?
    Answer: It represents deliberation as an open future containing alternative paths, with it being up to the agent which path becomes actual.

24. Question: What does AO require?
    Answer: If an action is free, the agent must have been able to refrain from that action or perform an alternative.

25. Question: What is an untouchable fact? Give examples from van Inwagen.
    Answer: It is a fact no human has ever had power to change, regardless of knowledge or luck. Examples include the earth's being round, magnets attracting iron, the existence of dinosaurs, and 317's being prime.

26. Question: Why does the Untouchability Principle seem plausible?
    Answer: If no one can affect p and no one can affect the fact that p guarantees q, it is difficult to see how anyone could affect q, which is the inevitable consequence.

27. Question: What does the Consequence Argument aim to establish?
    Answer: It aims to establish incompatibilism by showing that, if determinism is true, present actions are untouchable consequences of the untouchable distant past and laws of nature.

28. Question: Why is the Consequence Argument's conclusion relevant to free will?
    Answer: If being in class is an untouchable fact, no one has ever had the power to change it. The student therefore could not have done otherwise, so AO says the action was not free.

29. Question: Why is C2 of the classroom Consequence Argument defended as untouchable?
    Answer: The relevant state occurred millions of years before humans existed, so no human being had power over it; the laws of nature are also outside human control.

30. Question: Why is C3 defended as untouchable?
    Answer: Assuming determinism, the distant state plus the laws guarantee the later classroom fact, and no human has power to change that law-governed consequence.

31. Question: What kind of action might a libertarian treat as free?
    Answer: A psychologically torn choice in which more than one option is genuinely possible—for example, choosing between moral duty and self-interest—and the outcome is not determined by the past and laws.

32. Question: What is an SFA, and why is it important to Kane?
    Answer: An SFA is an undetermined, psychologically torn choice through which an agent forms character. Later determined actions can be attributable to the agent because earlier SFAs helped create the character from which they flow.

33. Question: How does Kane's businesswoman illustrate an SFA?
    Answer: She has strong competing reasons to help an assault victim and to reach an important meeting. She makes efforts in both directions; whichever choice succeeds expresses one set of her reasons and helps form the person she becomes.

34. Question: Does indeterminism make Kane's SFA merely accidental?
    Answer: Kane argues no. Indeterminism creates an obstacle within the agent's own conflicted will, but either resulting choice can still be voluntary, intentional, rational, and caused by the agent's effort.

35. Question: What follows if free will is incompatible with both determinism and indeterminism?
    Answer: Either determinism is true or it is false; if both possibilities rule out free will, then free will does not exist.

36. Question: What do libertarian and hard incompatibilists disagree about?
    Answer: They agree that determinism rules out free will. They disagree over whether free will exists and thus whether determinism is false, or whether free will is absent under either determinism or indeterminism.

37. Question: What is the harmful consequences objection to hard incompatibilism?
    Answer: It claims that accepting the denial of moral responsibility would erase morality, undermine punishment, release dangerous criminals, or otherwise harm society.

38. Question: Does the denial of moral responsibility imply that nothing is right or wrong?
    Answer: No. Pereboom distinguishes judgments that conduct is right or wrong from judgments that an agent deserves blame or punishment for it.

39. Question: Why does Pereboom reject retributivism?
    Answer: Retributivism justifies punishment by desert, but hard incompatibilism denies the responsibility that would make criminals deserve suffering for its own sake.

40. Question: How does the contagion model answer the worry that dangerous criminals must be released?
    Answer: Society may restrain a dangerous person to prevent harm, much as it may quarantine a carrier of disease, while recognizing that the restraint is preventive rather than deserved suffering and must respect proportionality and humane treatment.

41. Question: What happens in the Myth of Sisyphus, and why does it symbolize meaninglessness?
    Answer: Sisyphus endlessly pushes a stone uphill, it rolls back, and nothing lasting results. The cycle represents activity that repeatedly comes to nothing.

42. Question: What is the class's argument for pessimism about life's meaning?
    Answer: **NEEDS CONFIRMATION: the draft guide names this argument, but the supplied lecture-note PDF does not include its premises or exact classroom formulation.** Taylor does compare human life to Sisyphus because achievements are temporary and cycles continue, but that comparison should not be substituted for the missing class reconstruction without confirmation.

43. Question: Where does meaning come from according to Taylor?
    Answer: Taylor ultimately locates meaning in the agent's own will and inner drive to pursue an activity, not in a final achievement that permanently ends striving.

44. Question: What does Sisyphus on Drugs show?
    Answer: Once Sisyphus intensely wants to roll stones, the same objectively fruitless activity gains meaning for him. Taylor uses the case to show that meaning can come from the activity's answering the agent's own will.

45. Question: Why does Taylor think a final completed goal may not provide the best meaning?
    Answer: Completion followed by nothing but eternal contemplation would produce boredom. The building or striving, rather than the finished product alone, gives life its lived meaning.

46. Question: What is Wolf's positive account of meaning in life?
    Answer: A meaningful life is actively and at least somewhat successfully engaged in projects of positive value—what she elsewhere summarizes as subjective attraction meeting objective attractiveness.

47. Question: Why is subjective fulfillment insufficient for Wolf?
    Answer: A person may feel absorbed by a useless or worthless project. If feeling alone were enough, a pill that created the belief that life is meaningful could create meaning without changing the life, which Wolf rejects.

48. Question: Why is objective value insufficient for Wolf?
    Answer: A person may perform valuable activity while feeling alienated or disengaged. Meaning requires that the person be actively involved, not merely connected to something worthwhile from the outside.

49. Question: How do Taylor and Wolf differ about meaning?
    Answer: Taylor emphasizes the inner will to pursue an activity and treats that subjective drive as the source of meaning. Wolf requires both subjective engagement and a project with positive value beyond its merely seeming worthwhile to the agent.

50. Question: Does Wolf equate meaningfulness with moral goodness?
    Answer: No. A project can contribute to meaning through artistic, intellectual, athletic, or other positive value even when it is not a morally admirable project.

## Arguments

### Argument Title: The Consequence Argument

Short Title: Consequence

Source: Peter van Inwagen / lecture formulation

Steps:

1. Assume determinism is true.
2. It is an untouchable fact that the distant state of the world and the laws of nature occurred (DINO).
3. It is an untouchable fact that if DINO occurred, then I am in PHI 2010 on July 28, 2026.
4. Therefore, it is an untouchable fact that I am in PHI 2010 on July 28, 2026.
5. Therefore, if determinism is true, it is an untouchable fact that I am in PHI 2010 on July 28, 2026.

### Argument Title: The No Free Will Argument

Short Title: No Free Will

Source: Hard incompatibilism / lecture notes

Steps:

1. Either determinism is true or determinism is false.
2. If determinism is true, then we have no free will.
3. If determinism is false, then we have no free will.
4. Therefore, we have no free will.

### Argument Title: The Libertarian Argument

Short Title: Libertarianism

Source: Robert Kane / lecture notes

Steps:

1. If determinism is true, then we have no free will.
2. We do have free will.
3. Therefore, determinism is false.

### Argument Title: The Untouchable Facts Illustration

Short Title: Alpha Centauri

Source: Untouchability Principle / lecture notes

Steps:

1. It is an untouchable fact that fusion occurs in Alpha Centauri A.
2. It is an untouchable fact that if fusion occurs in Alpha Centauri A, then it produces light.
3. Therefore, it is an untouchable fact that Alpha Centauri A produces light.

### Argument Title: The Punishment Objection to Hard Incompatibilism

Short Title: Punishment Objection

Source: Harmful consequences discussion / Pereboom

Steps:

1. If hard incompatibilism is true, criminals are not morally responsible for their crimes.
2. If criminals are not morally responsible for their crimes, it is unjust to punish them.
3. Therefore, if hard incompatibilism is true, it is unjust to punish criminals.

### Argument Title: Pereboom's Contagion Reply

Short Title: Quarantine Reply

Source: Derk Pereboom / lecture formulation

Steps:

1. A dangerous person may pose a serious threat without being morally responsible for being dangerous.
2. Society may quarantine a nonresponsible carrier of a serious disease to protect others.
3. Relevant restraint must be preventive, proportionate, and humane rather than retributive.
4. Therefore, society may restrain dangerous criminals to prevent harm without claiming that they deserve suffering.

### Argument Title: The JoJo Argument Against Unsupplemented Deep Self Views

Short Title: JoJo

Source: Susan Wolf, "Sanity and the Metaphysics of Responsibility"

Steps:

1. Unsupplemented Deep Self Views say an agent is responsible when the deep self controls the will.
2. JoJo's deep self controls and endorses his brutal will.
3. JoJo is not morally responsible because his upbringing prevents him from properly recognizing right and wrong.
4. Therefore, deep-self control alone is not sufficient for responsibility.
5. Therefore, a sanity condition must be added to the Deep Self View.

### Argument Title: Kane's Self-Forming Action Reply to the Luck Objection

Short Title: SFA Control

Source: Robert Kane, "Reflections on Free Will"

Steps:

1. In a self-forming action, the agent has competing motives and makes an effort toward each option.
2. Indeterminism prevents either effort from being guaranteed to succeed.
3. Whichever effort succeeds expresses the agent's own reasons and effort.
4. The agent could voluntarily, intentionally, and rationally have chosen either option.
5. Therefore, an undetermined self-forming action need not be a mere accident or loss of agency.

### Argument Title: Taylor's Initial Argument from Sisyphus

Short Title: Sisyphus Pessimism

Source: Richard Taylor, "The Meaning of Life"

Steps:

1. Sisyphus's repeated labor produces no lasting culmination and always returns to its starting point.
2. Activity that endlessly comes to nothing is meaningless.
3. Human lives likewise consist of recurring striving whose achievements eventually pass away.
4. Therefore, viewed from the outside, human life resembles Sisyphus's meaningless cycle.

### Argument Title: Taylor's Subjective Meaning Reply

Short Title: Meaning from Within

Source: Richard Taylor, "The Meaning of Life"

Steps:

1. A final culmination can leave an agent with nothing meaningful to do and produce endless boredom.
2. In the altered Sisyphus case, Sisyphus deeply wants to roll stones.
3. His activity then fulfills the very desire that moves him, even though no lasting product results.
4. The building or striving can matter more to the agent than the finished product.
5. Therefore, life's meaning comes from within—from the will to pursue the activity.

### Argument Title: Wolf's Bipartite Account of Meaning

Short Title: Projects of Worth

Source: Susan Wolf, "Meanings of Lives"

Steps:

1. Mere passivity lacks active engagement and is not meaningful.
2. Alienated valuable activity lacks subjective engagement and is not fully meaningful.
3. Engaged activity directed at a worthless project lacks objective value and is not fully meaningful.
4. Therefore, neither subjective engagement nor objective value is sufficient alone.
5. Therefore, a meaningful life is actively and at least somewhat successfully engaged in projects of positive value.
